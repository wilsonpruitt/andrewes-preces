                *A Short Prayer after Confession.*

O my Saviour, Christ, my Saviour,
    Who shall grant me that I may die
        before I offend thee again,
Christ my Saviour, O my Saviour?
    O Lord, let a new manner of life

<!-- movement -->

                *A Preparatory Prayer.*

Lord, my heart is ready:
        (so saith the Psalmist,   *Ps.* 108:1.)
But, Lord, I fear that mine is not;
    Desire it I surely do, and grieve if it be less.
Would God it were ready!  woe is me, that it is not!
    Lord, I dispose myself, and prepare.
Help my disposing, and supply my preparation.
    I will set my sins before me,
        That they be not before thee.

## Translator's flags

1. **The sentence crosses the page-turn and the English crosses it too.** *…keeping a perpetual sabbath* (379) / *from sin and from its Occasion, Fuel, Peril* (380). **`Fomite` is rendered "Fuel"** — the *fomes peccati*, the tinder or kindling of sin left in us after baptism, a term of art in the schools. "Incitement" or "temptation" would blur it into the neighbouring *Occasion*; "tinder" is exact but archaic to the point of obscurity, and "fuel" keeps both the technical sense and the fire.

2. **⚠ The epigram's snap depends on the borrowed verb, and the English borrows it too.** *For as penitence destroyeth old sins, / So new sins destroy penitence.* The Latin's second limb has no verb — it takes *destruit* across the comma — and the English keeps the parallel tight enough that the reversal lands. It is the last line of the prayer and should read as one.

3. **⚠ "Who shall grant me that I may die before I offend thee again" is a request to die, and is left as one.** Not softened into a prayer for perseverance, not bracketed, not glossed. Andrewes means it in the way §29's *defunctis requiem* was meant — whatever is to be said about such a petition belongs in an introduction, not in the translation of it.

4. **`Servator` is rendered "Saviour" four times, in the printed order.** *O my Saviour, Christ, my Saviour … Christ my Saviour, O my Saviour?* — a chiasm wrapped around the petition. The temptation is to vary it ("my Redeemer", "my Deliverer") for English ears; the repetition **is** the prayer, and varying it would translate the words while destroying the act.

5. **⚠ The four-verb frame appears here a third time, and is worded to match §§34 and 37.** *I **fear** that mine is not; **Desire** it I surely do, and **grieve** if it be less.* §34 (printed 367) printed the frame empty; §37 head VI (printed 378) filled it; here three of the four verbs return in a third setting. Consistent English across all three lets a reader see it is a habit of Andrewes' mind, not a one-off table. **No objects are carried between the three passages.**

6. **`(so saith the Psalmist, Ps. 108:1.)` is kept as an aside, not made into a reference tag.** He quotes *My heart is ready* — Coverdale's wording at Ps. 108:1 — and then in the very next line doubts it of himself. The parenthesis is the hinge: the Psalmist could say it; can I?

7. **⚠ `I will set my sins before me, that they be not before thee` keeps *before* in both lines.** Ps. li. 3, *my sin is ever before me*, answered by Ps. li. 9, *hide thy face from my sins* — and the answer works only because the same preposition governs both. Rendering the second half "that thou mayest not see them" would state the sense and lose the argument, which is the entire logic of confession compressed into two lines.

8. **`Adjuva dispositionem, et præparationem meam supple` — "Help my disposing, and supply my preparation."** *Dispono me, et præparo* two lines above is what he can do; *adjuva* and *supple* are what he cannot. The pair of verbs is deliberate (help what I have begun, make up what I lack) and matches §37's *adauge, quem habeo / supple, quem non habeo* at printed 376 — same verb, same admission.

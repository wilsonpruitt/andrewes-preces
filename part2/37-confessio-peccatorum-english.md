                THE CONFESSION OF SINS.

    I. Behold me, Lord,
        Behold me, of sinners { the Greatest, / the Worst, / the Most wretched ! }
(And what shall I now say, or wherein shall I open my mouth?
What shall I answer, seeing I myself have done it, have done it, have done it?)
    [Is. 38:15]

<!-- printed 373 (PDF 400) -->

I will go over all my sins before thee
  In the bitterness of my soul:
    O that it were in bitterness most bitter.   [Ibid. and ver. 17]
Lord, if a man so live, and the life of my spirit be in such things,
    thou wilt correct me and make me to live.   [ver. 16]
For behold, in my peace
    my bitterness was made most bitter.   [ver. 17]
But I, like a swallow's young, will chatter,
    I will mourn as a dove.   [ver. 14]
And I beseech thee, Lord, by all thy mercy,
  That thy most just indignation and thy wrath be turned from me,
    Because I have sinned, and that grievously, I have sinned against thee,
      Grievously and often have I sinned against thee.
II. O Father of mercies,
        I beseech the fatherly bowels of thy compassions,
    And { an unclean worm, / a dead dog, / a putrid carcase, } O despise me not.
Nay rather look upon me, Lord,
  Look with those eyes
    wherewith thou lookedst on { the Magdalen at the feast, / Peter in the hall, / the Thief on the gibbet; }
    That with { Peter I may weep, / the Thief confess, / the Magdalen love; }
        That I may love thee much,
        Yea most, as one to whom most is forgiven.
Spare, I pray thee, Lord; spare, spare the penitent;

<!-- printed 374 (PDF 401) -->

    Surely { him that is minded and desirous to repent; / him that thinketh over his sins with bitterness, / him that is indignant with himself for the sins he hath done, / him that remembereth thy most bitter Passion, / and cleaveth unto it. }
Spare, Lord; be merciful.
  Spare me, Lord, be merciful unto me;
    Have mercy upon me,
      Seeing it { is not hard to thy power, / nor unbecoming to thy justice, / nor unwonted to thy clemency. }
III. What, for leeks and for garlick,
            the Bread of Angels?
  What, for the husks of swine,
        my Father's table?
    Woe is me, wretched, woe is me, senseless!
        Who hath bewitched me
            that I should so dote?
O if thou wouldest but vouchsafe to receive me,
  I am wholly minded to return:
    For then was it better with me than now.
Wholly confounded therefore,
    { Not worthy to name / Nor to call upon / Nor to think in my heart } thy Name
        but for thy inborn goodness;
Yet trusting in that,
    { a suppliant, / abased, / prostrate, } I return unto thee;

Nor do I ask that thou do
    aught but what thou hast done most often,
        And doest most willingly;
Which unless thou wert to do again and again,
    No flesh should stand,
    None should abide it.
Have mercy upon a sinner, upon the greatest of sinners,
  And therefore upon one that needeth the greatest mercy.
And thine is the greatest,
  And reacheth even unto the heavens,
    And plucketh out of the nethermost hell.
      Marvellous it is.
Shew thy marvellous mercy toward me:
  Which if thou wilt glorify beyond measure,
    Hold it out to me.
Never, never in the pardon of any sinner
        More glorious { either was it, / or shall it be. }
Lord, if thou wilt that I depart from thee, give me another Thee,
    Else will I not let thee go.
  Let the Spirit of truth teach me the truth.
IV. To thee, Lord, I confess
  (Because, if I would, I cannot hide them);
    To thee my sins, most many, most great, most grievous.
I profess also that I grieve for them, which thou too knowest.
  But that I have sinned more than I grieve,
    which grief lieth by me, that I might weep for my sins.
So great a grief I lack, I lack it utterly.
Far am I from being what it is meet I should be.
    Much can I sin;

    Much can I not grieve.
My drought, my drought! woe is me!
I cannot much, but I desire much.
For I know that not much is much enough.
    Would God that grief were at hand for me;
        yea, would God a greater.
    But I cannot obtain it of myself.
  I am dried up, dried up like a potsherd.
        Woe is me!
Thou, Lord, { increase that which I have, / supply that which I have not, } a fountain of tears,
        a melting heart, groanings that cannot be uttered.
Meanwhile, seeing the spirit is willing with me,
    Accept thou me according to that I have,
        Not according to that I have not.
But I will lengthen it out, since I cannot strain it further,
        through all the years of my life.
V. But having so often fallen back, with what face, with what mind
            shall I now be able to return?
        With none.  For wholly confounded,
    Covered with mine own confusion, I { walk, / sit, / lie down. }
Nor durst I do it,
    Nor would I, but that I should despair outright,
    And do that which they do that despair,
Save that there is yet hope left.
        What hope?
That unto seventy times seven
    Thou reachest forth thy mercy.

        And beyond,
For this is the measure thou hast commended to us.
    To us, that we should render it among ourselves,
        And thou thyself not render the same?
    Nay, thou wilt render it, and much more.
God forbid thou shouldest will more perfection to be in us
            than is in thyself.
    That thou shouldest will us to forgive unto seventy times seven,
      And thyself be unwilling.
For thy mercy surpasseth ours,
        as far as thou surpassest us.
Trusting therefore in that,
  yea, in thy mercy that pardoneth four hundred and ninety
        times over,
    Standing afar off I cast myself down,
    And most abjectly, as is meet, and most humbly
        Smiting that heart of mine
    (Which smiteth me not enough),
{ I say / and say again } over and over,
Be merciful, { Lord, to me a sinner, / to me the most wretched sinner, / to me the chief of sinners, / to me that am all sin, / to sin exceeding sinful, }
O thou to whom none ever sueth without hope of pardon!
VI. But that he should be merciful, and thou not grieve?
            I ask it not.
        For grieve I do, after a fashion;
        I fear indeed that it is but enough.

        I would desire it were more;
        I should rejoice if it were more;
        I grieve that it is not more.
        For I desire that I might more, and
            I grieve that more I cannot.
        I confess my grief is to be grieved for,
            And I grieve myself that it is so much to be grieved for.
    And, O who shall give me that I might more and more?
        I, if it lay with me, would perform it.
            But with me it lieth not;
It is not in my power.
    With me indeed it lieth to prize that which is more;
            yea, to will that it were more.
    To will is present with me; but to perform I find not.
Thou, Lord, give: with thee it lieth to give.
    Thou that turnest even the flint into standing water.
Give tears; give a fountain to my head.
        Give the grace of tears.
    Drop down, ye heavens, from above,
    And water the drought of my desert.
Give, Lord, this grace,
No gift were welcomer to me,
        Not great riches,
            Not earthly things, no, not the best;
Than if thou give me tears;
    Such as thou grantedst David of old, or Jeremiah:
    Such as thou grantedst the Magdalen, or Peter.
At least a dropping eye,
        that I be not all flint.
Not that I may wash my bed,

<!-- printed 379 (PDF 406), upper -->

    Not that I may water thy feet,
    Not copiously, as Jeremiah,
    Not bitterly, as Peter,
            (Though O, that it were so!)
Yet one little tear at the least
            And a second supply thou;
    Which thou mayest lay up in thy bottle,
    Which thou mayest enter in thy book.
But if I obtain not even this — ah, pumice-stone!
    Ah me, very quicklime! seething in cold water,
    Without, where it less behoveth, without fervour,
    Where it behoveth not, grieving enough,
    Where it most behoveth, cold, dry, dead,
At least of the tears of thy Christ
    Impart to me some,
Which in the days of his flesh he shed abundantly.
Impart, O impart to me of them.
    There is left over in him for me what in myself is lacking to me.

## Translator's flags

1. **⚠ The section is Hezekiah's psalm turned into a confession, and head I changes the person to do it.** Is. xxxviii. 15 in the Vulgate asks *what shall I answer, when **He** hath done it?* — the sick king bewildered at what God has sent. Andrewes writes *cum ipse **fecerim, fecerim, fecerim***: when **I myself** have done it, three times. The English keeps the triple, keeps the first person, and does not repair the quotation. The chapter goes on supplying the section's language for the next page (the bitterness, the chattering swallow, the mourning dove, *in my peace my bitterness was most bitter*).

2. **`Recogitabo Tibi omnia peccata mea` — "all my sins," where Isaiah has "all my years."** The Vulgate's *omnes annos meos* becomes *omnia peccata mea*. Same hand as flag 1; not normalised toward the AV's "I shall go softly all my years."

3. **⚠ Head VI fills in the four-cell table that §34 left blank, and the English is worded to let that be seen.** §34 (printed 367) prints only the frame — *I would, if more. | I fear, lest. / I grieve, that not. | I should rejoice, if.* Here the same four verbs come with their objects: *I fear indeed that it is but enough. / **I would desire it were more**; / **I should rejoice if it were more**; / **I grieve that it is not more.*** I deliberately did not complete §34's table there; **Andrewes completes it himself, eleven pages later**, and the English of the four verbs is identical in both places so the reader can watch the frame being filled.

4. **⚠ `Non amare, ut Petrus` = "Not BITTERLY, as Peter" — the adverb, not the verb "to love."** Luke xxii. 62, *flevit amare*, "he wept bitterly." It stands fourth in a list of weepings (David washing his bed, Ps. vi. 6; the Magdalen watering his feet; Jeremiah's copious tears; Peter's bitter ones), and "not to love, as Peter" would read perfectly smoothly and be perfectly wrong.

5. **⚠ `vere calcem! in aqua frigida ferventem` is rendered "very quicklime! seething in cold water."** Lime boils when cold water is thrown on it, and is dead cold otherwise: the man is fervent where he should not be (*without, where it less behoveth*) and *cold, dry, dead* where he most should burn. Without the lime the four lines are a complaint; with it they are a diagnosis. "Lime" alone would be read as the fruit — **quicklime** is necessary.

6. **The Publican's prayer is a five-rung ladder and the English climbs it.** *Lord, to me a sinner* (Luke xviii. 13) → *to me the most wretched sinner* → *to me the chief of sinners* (1 Tim. i. 15) → *to me that am all sin* → **to sin exceeding sinful** (Rom. vii. 13, καθ᾽ ὑπερβολήν — Paul's own phrase, which is why it is in Greek). *Mihi toti peccato* is not "to me, wholly a sinner": he has stopped being a man who sins and become the thing itself.

7. **`quadringenties nonagies` is given as "four hundred and ninety times over."** It is seventy times seven reckoned up (Matt. xviii. 22), set two lines after *seventy times seven* and *And beyond* — the arithmetic is the joke and the comfort together, and "many times" would erase both.

8. **⚠ `da mihi alium Te` — "give me another Thee."** Jacob at Peniel (*I will not let thee go*) made into an argument: if thou wilt have me go from thee, then provide me another Thee to go to; otherwise I stay. **Not** "another like thee," **not** "someone else" — the audacity is the point, and it is the boldest sentence in Part II so far.

9. **The three attributes at 374 are all kept.** *It is not hard to thy **power**, nor unbecoming to thy **justice**, nor unwonted to thy **clemency*** — the argument is that pardoning him offends none of the three, so collapsing them into "it is not hard for thee" would remove the reasoning.

10. **Internal echoes rendered with identical wording, on purpose.** (a) 374's *not worthy to name, nor to call upon, nor to think in my heart* repeats §34's line at printed 366; (b) 375's *No flesh should stand, none should abide it* repeats §36's two proofs at printed 371 (Ps. cxxx. 3 and cxliii. 2, in Coverdale's words both times); (c) the Magdalen/Peter/Thief triad of 373 returns at 378 among the four weepers. Andrewes is quoting himself, and the translation lets him.

11. **`Et ne { … } ne me despice` keeps its double negative shape** — the *ne* stands before the brace and again after it in the Latin. English cannot repeat it twice without absurdity, so the emphasis is carried by putting *O despise me not* after the three members, where the second *ne* is.

12. **`Extendam autem, quia intendere non possum magis` — "I will lengthen it out, since I cannot strain it further."** A deliberate pair: he cannot make his grief more intense, so he will make it longer — *through all the years of my life*. The verbs are *intendere* (to stretch tighter) and *extendere* (to stretch longer), and the English keeps them as a pair of stretchings rather than reaching for "deepen."

13. **`Supersunt in illo mihi, quæ in me mihi desunt` closes the section on a balance and the English keeps the balance** — *there is left over in him for me what in myself is lacking to me*. Four words do the work twice over (*supersunt/desunt*, *in illo/in me*), and a smoother English ("he supplies what I lack") would spend the sentence's whole capital.

## Translator's flags

1. **ἀφορμαί rendered "OCCASIONS," and the choice defended.** The word means a starting-point, a base of operations, a handle, the thing one takes off from — not a "theme" and not a "material." **"Occasions of Meditation" is the phrase the period itself used** (Joseph Hall's *Occasional Meditations*, 1630, is contemporary with Andrewes' circle), and it carries exactly the right sense: these are not the meditation but what gives rise to it. "Springboards," "starting-points," and "handles" are all accurate and all wrong in register. The word is kept in the same position in the heading each time even though **the 1853 varies the Greek/Latin word-order across the three headings** (see transcript flag 3) — English cannot show that variation, and it is not worth an editorial note in the text.

2. **⚠ The Hebrew is kept, then Englished, then its own Latin gloss is Englished after it — three things on one line, and none may be dropped.** לך דומיה תהלה (Ps. lxv. 2 Hebrew / lxv. 1 English Bibles) says literally **"to thee silence is praise"** — praise, before thee, is silence. The 1853 then gives Andrewes' Latin, *tacet tibi laus vel Psalmus* ("praise is silent unto thee, or the Psalm is"), which is a **literal Hebrew rendering set deliberately against the Vulgate's *Te decet hymnus*** and against the Prayer Book's "Thou, O God, art praised in Sion." **The BCP reading must NOT be used here**, notwithstanding §9's Coverdale-first rule: this is the one kind of case the rule cannot cover, because Andrewes is quoting the Hebrew precisely where the Greek-and-Latin tradition went the other way, and is then arguing from the difference on the next page (*plus habet silentii*). §9 is a crib rule for psalm-lines Andrewes took from the LXX/Vulgate; when he reaches past them to the Hebrew, the Hebrew governs. **Second override of §9 in Part II** (the first was §28's *κατ᾽ ἐμοῦ*), and for the same underlying reason.

3. **The 384 gloss belongs to the Hebrew, not to Augustine** — *i. e. Opera Tua non attingit, plus habet silentii; et videtur procedere ex ore lactentium* explains why silence is praise: our praise **does not reach thy works**, so there is more silence in it than speech, and what little comes out is babble — *out of the mouth of sucklings*, Ps. viii. 2. The English keeps "sucklings" (Coverdale's "babes and sucklings") rather than "infants," because the psalm is being quoted, and keeps the impersonal *videtur* ("it seemeth") rather than resolving it to "our praise seems."

4. **`Non passibus, sed precibus, itur ad Deum` — the sound-pun is carried as far as English allows.** *Passibus/precibus* is a near-rhyme doing the whole work of the line. "Not by paces, but by prayers, is the way to God" keeps the alliteration and keeps the impersonal construction (*itur*, "one goes / the going is done"), which matters: the saying is a rule about how anyone gets there, not a report about us. "We do not come to God by walking" would be accurate and dead.

5. **`Fides fundit orationem, et fundatur in oratione` also puns, on two different verbs that look like one.** *Fundo, fundere* = to pour out; *fundo, fundare* = to found. Faith **pours out** prayer and is **founded** upon prayer. English cannot make the two words look alike, and the attempt ("faith founts prayer and is founded…") is worse than the loss. Rendered plainly, flagged here.

6. **`Ascendit precatio, descendit miseratio` — kept to four words if possible.** "Prayer goeth up, pity cometh down." The Latin is two verbs and two nouns in perfect chiasmus of direction, and any English that adds a subject pronoun or an "and" slackens it. This is the most quotable line in the first catena and should be set as an epigram in print.

7. **The three closing epigrams of the Intercession catena carry the section's argument and must stay tight.** Especially the middle one: *Si pro te solo rogas, rogabis pro te solus* turns on **solo → solus**, the ablative of the thing prayed for becoming the nominative of the man praying. "If thou ask for thyself alone, thou shalt be alone in asking for thyself" keeps the pivot; "if you only pray for yourself, only you will pray for you" says it and loses it. The third (*orabunt pro te*) has no expressed subject — **"they shall pray for thee," not "all shall pray for thee"** — because the Latin lets the *omnibus* of the line before do the work, and so should the English.

8. **`John` iii. 27 is short of the AV, and stays short.** AV reads "except it be given him **from heaven**"; Andrewes' Greek has only *ἐὰν μὴ ᾖ δεδομένον*. The English follows Andrewes and drops the words the AV supplies — his abbreviations are part of what the edition is for.

9. **`Luke` xxi. 36 attaches "at every season" to the WATCHING, not to the praying.** AV puts *always* with *pray* ("watch ye therefore, and pray always"); Andrewes' Greek reads Ἀγρυπνεῖτε **ἐν παντὶ καιρῷ**, δεόμενοι ἵνα… — watch at every season, praying that… The English follows the Greek as printed. Likewise the AV's "all these things" is not in his text and is not supplied.

10. **`1 Sam.` xii. 23 keeps Andrewes' *before the Lord* / *before him*, against the AV's *against the LORD*.** His Greek reads *τὸ ἁμαρτεῖν ἐνώπιον τοῦ Κυρίου* and *ἐνώπιον αὐτοῦ ὑπὲρ ὑμῶν*, where the LXX and the AV have "sin against the Lord" and "pray for you." **The change is doctrinally live** — to cease interceding is a sin committed *in God's presence*, not merely an offence against him — and the doubled *ἐνώπιον* is what makes it; the English repeats "before" in both places for that reason, at some cost in smoothness.

11. **`Psal.` lii. 10 is translated from the received sense, not from the plate's grammar.** The plate reads *ἐναντίων* (genitive plural) where the LXX has adverbial *ἐναντίον*; see transcript flag 6. The Greek is left as printed and unrepaired there, and the English gives "in the sight of thy saints," which is what the line has always meant. **The English does not signal the slip on the page** — it is a compositor's error, not a reading of Andrewes'.

12. **`Psal.` lxxxix. 16 — "he," not "the people," and the plural kept.** Andrewes drops the LXX's *ὁ λαός*, so the beatitude falls on the man praying rather than on Israel, and pluralises *ἀλαλαγμόν* to *ἀλαλάγματα*. Rendered "Blessed is he that knoweth the joyful sounds" — the AV's "joyful sound" is the received phrase and is kept in substance, but the plural is Andrewes' and stands. Coverdale's "that can rejoice in thee" is further off and is not used here.

13. **`Aug. Conf.` — "keep silence of thee," not "are silent about thee."** *Væ tacentibus de Te* is Confessions I. iv; the English wants the biblical construction (to keep silence *of* a thing) so that the paradox in the second half lands: those who say nothing are woeful, **and yet those who talk are dumb anyway** — *etiam loquaces muti sunt*. "Talkative" is deliberately unflattering; *loquax* is not a compliment in Augustine.

14. **`Baruch` iv. 28 says "double," not the AV's "ten times more."** The plate reads διπλασιάσατε (verified at 600 dpi); the LXX and the AV read δεκαπλασιάσατε / "ten times more." The English follows the plate — and Andrewes' own Latin line immediately beneath does the same work anyway, *as before in sins, so now in good works*, which is a doubling, not a tenfolding.

15. **⚠ The last line is a CUE and keeps its dash.** *Ἀλλὰ τίς εἰμι ἐγώ, Κύριε—&c.* = 2 Sam. vii. 18, and Andrewes is pointing the reader back to a text **already set out in full in §25** (printed 348foot–349, in its cento with 2 Sam. ix. 8). "But who am I, O Lord?—&c." is the whole of it. **Do not complete the quotation here** — that would make §40 end on a sentence when it ends on a gesture, and would duplicate §25. Cross-referenced in both sections' files with a do-not-import warning, as with §34's four-cell table.

16. **`Perge igitur ἀγωνίζεσθαι ἐν προσευχαῖς` is one line in English as it is one line in the plate.** Col. iv. 12, where Paul says Epaphras is *always striving for you in prayers*; Andrewes turns the participle into an imperative aimed at the reader. **"Strive," the athletic word** (ἀγών, the contest), not "labour" and not "wrestle" — "wrestling in prayer" is a later idiom and would date the line to the wrong century.

17. **`unam Columbam` is the Church as the one Dove** (Cant. vi. 9, *una est columba mea*), and is capitalised in the English as the plate capitalises it. Augustine's argument only works if the reader hears that the many groaning saints are one bird; "one dove" in lower case reads as a simile.

## Translator's flags

1. **The two opening Greek lines are unreferenced in the source, and the English supplies both.** Gen. 24:12 — Abraham's servant praying at the well of Nahor, LXX *εὐόδωσον ἐναντίον ἐμοῦ σήμερον*, AV "send me good speed this day"; and Exod. 33:15 — Moses, "If thy presence go not with me, carry us not up hence." **Without the references the prayer is opaque**, and with them its argument is plain: the servant who was sped on his journey, and the man who would not stir without the Presence. The Latin that follows picks up that same servant in its first clause.

2. **The four relative clauses have no verb, and the English does not give them one outright.** *Qui puerorum Abrahæ … Magorum iter … Qui Petrum … Paulum …* hang unresolved until *Adesto mihi, Domine*. Bracketed `[didst guide]` / `[didst uphold]` keep them readable while showing they are supplied; closing each into a finished sentence would destroy the suspension that makes the four examples one appeal.

3. **`Magorum iter | Stellæ` is a two-column echo in the printing** — an Angel led Abraham's servant, a Star the Magi — with *Stellæ* set off to the right beneath *ductu Angeli*. Kept as a column row so the pairing survives.

4. **`Conduc, et deduc, et reduc` — the line the prayer exists for.** Three prefixes on one root, naming the three parts of a journey: **lead me with thee** (*conduc*), **lead me forth** (*deduc*), **lead me home again** (*reduc*). English cannot do it in three words, so it does it in three phrases; what must not happen is three flat "leads," which would leave a traveller's prayer with no return in it.

5. **Ps. 68:1 is a travelling verse and is meant to be recognised.** "Let God arise, and let his enemies be scattered" is the Ark's own formula when it set forward (Num. 10:35); Coverdale's wording used per §9.

6. **`Scrutabor` rendered "I will search out," against Coverdale's "keep."** Ps. 119:115 in the BCP reads "Away from me, ye wicked: I will keep the commandments of my God," and §9 would ordinarily settle it — but *scrutabor* is the stronger word Andrewes actually has (to search into, examine), and the first half of the verse is already Coverdale's. The divergence is deliberate and noted.

7. **⚠ Both columns are received mnemonic verses, and their moods disagree on purpose.** The corporal seven — *Visito, poto, cibo, redimo, tego, colligo, condo* — are the standard memory-hexameter, **first person: I visit, I give drink, I feed, I ransom, I clothe, I harbour, I bury.** The spiritual seven — *Consule, plecte, doce, solare, remitte, fer, ora* — are the companion verse, **imperative: counsel, chastise, teach, comfort, forgive, bear, pray.** Andrewes is copying two existing verses, not composing a pair, so the English keeps both moods rather than regularising them into one. Keep the words bare; these are pegs for the memory, and a fluent English sentence would make them useless.

8. **`colligo` = I harbour** (Matt. 25:35, "I was a stranger and ye took me in"), not "I gather." **`condo` = I bury** — the seventh work, from Tobit, which is why the Gospel list of six is here a seven. **`tego`** covers clothing and sheltering at once; left as "I clothe," the primary sense, with the double meaning noted here rather than expanded in the line.

9. **`plecte` = chastise** in the sense of correcting the offender (the traditional *corripere peccantem*), not punishing him. Rendered "chastise" to keep the mnemonic's single word.

                A PRAYER OF THOMAS BRADWARDINE,

            Book I. Of Virtue, chap. i. coroll. 39.

  Thyself, my God, thyself for thine own sake above all things else do I love. Thyself I desire. Thyself, as my last end, I long after. Thyself for thine own sake, and no other thing whatsoever, alway and in all things I seek. Out of all mine inward parts and marrow, with groaning and with weeping, with continual labour, and with grief. What then wilt thou render me at the last? If thou render not thyself to me, thou renderest nothing. If thou give not thyself to me, thou givest nothing. If I find not thyself, I find nothing. To no purpose dost thou reward me; nay, thou dost torment me sore. For before ever I sought thee, I hoped at the last to find thee, and to hold thee. And with that honey-sweet hope I was sweetly comforted in all my labours. But now, if thou deny me thyself, whatsoever else thou givest — cheated of so great a hope, and that not for a little while, but for ever — shall I not alway languish for love, and for languishing mourn, and for mourning grieve, and for grief lament and weep, seeing I shall remain for ever empty and void? Shall I not be sorrowful past comfort? complain without ceasing? be tormented without end? This is not thy way, O most good, most merciful, most loving God; it nowise agreeth with thee, it nowhere becometh thee. Grant therefore, O most good my God, that in this present life I may alway love thyself for thine own sake above all things, and seek thee in all things, and at the last, in the life to come, find thee and hold thee for ever.

## Translator's flags

1. **⚠ This prayer is not Andrewes' and the page says so.** He copies out **Thomas Bradwardine**, Archbishop of Canterbury, dead some two hundred and seventy years, and prints his name and his book over it. The English keeps heading and citation as a heading and a citation — the borrowing is part of what is being said. *Nothing in the rendering makes it sound like the surrounding sections*; it is a fourteenth-century schoolman's Latin, argued rather than chanted, and it should read that way against the litanies on either side.

2. **The citation is translated but not corrected.** *Lib. I. De Virtute, cap. i. coroll. 39* → *Book I. Of Virtue, chap. i. coroll. 39*. The work is Bradwardine's *De Causa Dei contra Pelagium*; the 1853's way of naming it stands, per the standing rule on references.

3. **The three engines of the prayer, all kept in English.** (a) ***Teipsum*** stands first in its clause eight times and is rendered **"thyself"** in first position every time, even where English would rather say "thee" — the whole prayer is the difference between wanting God's gifts and wanting God. (b) The **cascade** *amore languebo, languore mœrebo, mœrore dolebo, dolore lugebo et flebo* is carried over as **"languish for love, and for languishing mourn, and for mourning grieve, and for grief lament and weep"** — each state becoming the cause of the next, which is the sentence's whole argument about a hope disappointed. (c) The three **-biliter** adverbs are kept as three parallel questions — *past comfort · without ceasing · without end*.

4. **`finaliter` twice, and rendered twice as an end, not as "finally."** *Teipsum **finaliter** concupisco* = I long for thee **as my last end**; *Quid ergo **finaliter** mihi reddes?* = what wilt thou render me **at the last**. The adverb is scholastic vocabulary (the *finis*, the end for which), and flattening it to "finally" turns a doctrine of the chief good into a filler word.

5. **⚠ The next section's heading is missing in the 1853, and the English of §36 begins as abruptly as the Latin.** Printed 370 stops a third of the way up the page; printed 371 opens in the middle of an intercession, *Pro spiritu vulneratis* — no title, no first petition. Confirmed present in the plate (consecutive leaves, OCR agrees). Nothing is supplied at either end; the gap is left visible and is on the watch list for restoration from Brightman/Harley before print.

6. **`Nequicquam me præmias, sed crucias vehementer` is rendered with its sting kept** — *To no purpose dost thou reward me; nay, thou dost torment me sore.* Bradwardine is saying that heaven's gifts without heaven's God would be a cruelty, and the *sed* is an accusation. Softening it ("thou dost but grieve me") would take the risk out of a prayer whose whole force is that it is willing to say this to God.

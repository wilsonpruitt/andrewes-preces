## Translator's flags

1. **⚠ This is the *Commendatio Animæ* — the deathbed prayer — put into Greek, and the English must let it be heard.** The Latin commendation of a departing soul runs *Libera, Domine, animam ejus, sicut liberasti Noe de diluvio, Abraham de Ur Chaldæorum, Isaac de hostia, Job de passionibus, tres pueros de camino ignis ardentis …* It is one of the oldest prayers in the Western books, and Andrewes has done three things to it: **turned it into Greek**, **carried the roll into the New Testament** (the disciples, Peter, Paul — where the received litany stops in the Old), and **changed whom it asks for**: not *his soul* but ***us***. Rendering it in flat modern English would hide all three. The repeated bare "N. from X" is the litany's own shape and is kept.

2. **The title-line is a refrain stated once.** *Sicut patres, et nos libera, Domine* — in the Latin litany the *sicut liberasti … libera* formula recurs after every name; Andrewes states it at the head and again at the foot (*So deliver us also, O Lord*) and lets the twenty-one names stand between. **The English keeps both ends identical in wording** so the reader can see the list is bracketed, not merely introduced.

3. **The preposition drops after David and the list accelerates.** In the Greek, *from* (ἀπό) is written out down to David and then disappears, the bare genitive carrying it the rest of the way. English cannot drop "from" without ungrammar, so the acceleration is carried instead by shortening: the later entries are given as tersely as English allows (**Esther from Haman · Joash from Athaliah · Jeremiah from the dungeon**), with no supplied articles or explanations.

4. **`Job from his temptations`** — *πειρασμοί* is the same word as in the Lord's Prayer's sixth petition and in the Latin litany's *Job de passionibus*. "Trials" would be smoother and would break the link with §31's *ne me sinas intrare in tentationem*; **temptations** keeps it.

5. **Keilah is a place.** In David's seven-fold brace — Saul, Goliath, **Keilah**, Ahithophel, Absalom, Doeg, Sheba — the third member is the city whose men would have delivered him up (1 Sam. 23), not a man. Left as the bare name, exactly as the Greek has it; a note, not a gloss in the line.

6. **`Moses from stoning`** is Exod. 17:4 ("they be almost ready to stone me"), not the Red Sea — the Red Sea belongs to *the people*, on the next line. Easy to mis-assign and worth stating.

7. **`Joseph from the slander of his mistress`** — *δεσποίνη* is the mistress of the house (Potiphar's wife); the brace pairs the slander with the prison it produced, so the two members are cause and consequence, not two separate escapes.

8. **`Paul from … the beast` is left ambiguous.** Either the viper at Malta (Acts 28:3–5) or the beasts at Ephesus (1 Cor. 15:32). The Greek singular does not settle it and neither does the English.

9. **`who hope in thee` is a participle attached to *us*, not a new clause.** *ἐλπίζοντας ἐπὶ Σοί* agrees with *ἡμᾶς* — deliver **us, who hope in thee**. The last two words are the ground of the whole list: the plea rests not on our being like the fathers but on the God being the same. Keeping it as a hanging participle preserves that; making it a sentence of its own would turn a reason into an afterthought.

10. **`Jeremiah from the dungeon`** — *βόθρος*, the miry pit of Jer. 38. "Pit" is the more literal word but "dungeon" is the AV's and carries the imprisonment; the mire is lost either way.

## Translator's flags

1. **The three vocatives are the Litany's, verbatim.** *Pater de cœlis Deus · Fili Redemptor mundi Deus · Spiritus Sancte Paraclete Deus* are the opening invocations of the Latin Litany, which the BCP Englishes as "O God the Father, of heaven · O God the Son, Redeemer of the world · O God the Holy Ghost." **Use the BCP's words** — a reader who knows the Litany must hear it here, because Andrewes is quoting a text his readers said aloud, not composing an address.

2. **Each Person gets exactly three relative clauses, and the structure is the prayer.** Father: creation from nothing, providence, the giving up of the Only-begotten (Rom. viii. 32). Son: incarnation of a Virgin, cleansing by blood (1 Jo. i. 7), resurrection-and-ascension as conqueror (Eph. iv. 8). Spirit: the Dove at the Jordan (Matt. iii. 16), the fiery tongues (Act. ii. 3), and — the one clause in the **present tense** — *qui corda Sanctorum Tua gratia visitas et confirmas*, the work still going on. **Keep that tense change**; it is why the Spirit's triad ends without a reference.

3. **`Beata, benedicta` are two different words and need two in English.** *Beata* = blessed as in happy/blissful (the Trinity's own beatitude); *benedicta* = blessed as in well-spoken-of (blessed by us). English has one word for both, so the pair must be split — "Happy, blessed Trinity" — and the loss noted.

4. **`semper laudabilis, semper tamen ineffabilis`** — the *tamen* is the hinge: ever to be praised, and **yet** ever unspeakable. Not "but"; the concession is the point of the whole adoration, that praise is owed to what praise cannot reach.

5. **`Pater bone, / Fili pie, / Spiritus benigne`** — three vocative adjectives, one per Person, all meaning something adjacent to "kind," and all three must stay distinct: *bone* (good), *pie* (merciful, dutiful-in-love), *benigne* (gracious, generous). *Pius* of the Son is the Latin word for covenant-faithfulness toward kin, not "pious."

6. **The two `Cujus` triads are printed as vertical braces** with *Cujus* set to the left of the brace, and are written inline here per the repo convention (`{ a / b / c }`), which collapses three printed lines into one — the standing practice since §5. The second triad is **nested under the first in the printing** (a smaller brace, indented), so the sense is: whose Majesty/Power/Goodness are such — **whose** work is life, love grace, contemplation glory.

7. **`Cujus opus vita, amor gratia, contemplatio gloria`** — three verbless equations. Supply "is" in English but keep them clipped; expanding them into clauses ("whose work is the giving of life") destroys the terseness that makes them a summary of the whole economy: to be made, to be loved, to see.

8. **⚠ `defunctis requiem et lucem perpetuam` — a prayer for the departed.** The words are the Requiem's own (*requiem æternam … et lux perpetua luceat eis*), in the private devotions of a Jacobean English bishop. **Translate it plainly — "to the departed rest and light perpetual" — and do not soften, gloss, or bracket it.** It is one of the theologically load-bearing lines in Part II and the edition's job is to print it, not to manage it.

9. **`quos futurum exutos corpore jam suscepit`** — *futurum* (sc. *sæculum*), the age to come, set against *præsens sæculum* on the line above; *exutos corpore* = "unclothed of the body" (2 Cor. v. 4, *nolumus expoliari*). The two relative clauses are a matched pair — those whom this age still holds in the flesh, those whom the next has already received — and the English must keep them parallel, because the four-fold *Da* that follows depends on the pairing.

10. **`Qui vivorum dominaris simul et mortuorum` (Rom. xiv. 9)** — AV "that he might be Lord both of the dead and living." *Simul* = alike, at once; the point is one lordship over both, which is the warrant for praying for both in the next lines.

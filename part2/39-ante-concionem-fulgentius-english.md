            *A Caution before Preaching, with a Prayer,*
                *out of Fulgentius, Bk* i, *to Monimus.*

  Let the preacher labour to be heard willingly, understandingly, and obediently. And let him not doubt that he can do this more by the piety of his prayers than by any faculty of speech. Praying for himself and for those he is to speak to, let him be one that prays before he is one that teaches; and drawing near devoutly, before ever he puts forth the tongue that is to utter, let him lift up his thirsting soul to God; that he may pour out what he has there drunk in, and shed forth what has filled him.

  Therefore I cease not to ask of our true Lord and Master that he would vouchsafe to teach me those things — whether by the words of his Scriptures, or by the conversation of the brethren, or by

<!-- printed 381 (PDF 408), upper -->

the inward and sweeter teaching of his own inspiration — which I may so set forth and so affirm that in my propositions and affirmations I may cleave alway to the truth: of this very Truth I ask to be taught the many things I know not, of whom I received the few things I know.

  Her I entreat that (by mercy going before me and following after) she would teach me whatsoever I know not that is needful to be known; keep me in those things which I truly know; correct me in those wherein, being a man, I am deceived; confirm me in those truths wherein I stagger, and deliver me from what is false and hurtful; and make those things to proceed out of my mouth which are chiefly pleasing before the Truth herself, that so they may be acceptable to all the faithful, through Jesus Christ our Lord and Saviour.

## Translator's flags

1. **⚠ The attribution is translated as printed and is NOT to be trusted without checking.** The 1853 tags this *ex Fulgentii l. i. ad Mon.* But *sit prius orator, quam doctor* is very close to **Augustine, *De Doctrina Christiana* IV**, and the two prayer-paragraphs read like **Augustine, *De Trinitate* I**. The tag may be composite or a misattribution inherited from the 1675 or from Andrewes himself. **I have not checked the text of Fulgentius, so this is a question and not a finding** — the heading stands exactly as printed in both layers, and the matter goes to the pre-print verification list. If it proves wrong, it belongs in a note, not in a mended line.

2. **⚠ `sit prius orator, quam doctor` — the pun is lost in English and the loss is declared here.** *Orator* means both **one who prays** and **a public speaker**; the sentence says "let him be an *orator* before he is a teacher" and means "let him be a **pray-er** first." The same doubling runs through the clause before it: his power lies *pietate **orationum**, quam **orationis** facultate* — by the piety of his **prayers** rather than the faculty of his **speech**, the same noun twice in two senses. English must choose, and I have chosen praying in both places ("one that prays", "the piety of his prayers"), because the argument is about prayer and only the joke is about oratory. A reader of the finished book should be told this once in a note.

3. **`ut eructet, quod inde biberit` — rendered "pour out," on the psalm's authority.** *Eructare* is physically "to belch," but it is the Vulgate's verb at Ps. xlv. 1 (*Eructavit cor meum verbum bonum* — Coverdale, "My heart is inditing of a good matter"), and in that register it carries no coarseness. What must survive is the **order**: he drinks first and pours after, which is the entire counsel — the preacher may only give out what he has taken in.

4. **The tongue comes second, in the Latin and in the English.** *Before ever he puts forth the tongue that is to utter, let him lift up his thirsting soul* — two matched participles, *proferentem linguam* against *animam sitientem*, in that order. Any rendering that reaches the tongue first has reversed the instruction it is translating.

5. **`præveniente ac subsequente misericordia` takes the Church's own words: "mercy going before me and following after."** This is prevenient and following grace, the language of the Articles and of the Collect *Prevent us, O Lord, in all our doings* — the Latin is technical, and paraphrase would make it sound like a flourish.

6. **⚠ `Ipsam rogo` — "Her I entreat." The Truth is being prayed to.** *Veritas* is feminine, and the passage slides across three sentences from *cleaving to the truth*, to *of this very Truth I ask*, to a direct petition addressed to **her** — that is, to Christ, who is the Truth. I have capitalised *Truth* from the point where the address begins, so the shift is visible; without that, a reader takes the last paragraph for a prayer to an abstraction, which is exactly what it is not.

7. **The final petition is kept as one sustained period**, as the Latin has it: teach me what I know not · keep me in what I know · correct me where I am deceived · confirm me where I stagger · deliver me from the false and hurtful · and make my mouth utter what pleases Truth herself, that it may be accepted by all the faithful. The prayer moves inward to outward — mind, mouth, hearers — and breaking it into short sentences would lose that movement.

8. **`quæ sunt coram ipsa veritate principaliter grata, sic fiant fidelibus cunctis accepta` keeps its two-step.** Pleasing **first** before the Truth, and **so** acceptable to the faithful: the order of approval is the point, and the *sic* is not merely connective. A preacher is not to seek what pleases his hearers and hope it also pleases God.

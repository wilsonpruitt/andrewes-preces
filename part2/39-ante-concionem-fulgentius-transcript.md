## Translator's flags

1. **⚠ THE ATTRIBUTION NEEDS CHECKING BEFORE PRINT — and is NOT corrected here.** The 1853 cites *ex Fulgentii l. i. ad Mon.* (Fulgentius of Ruspe, *Ad Monimum* I). But two things in this passage look strongly like **Augustine**: *sit prius **orator**, quam **doctor*** is very close to *De Doctrina Christiana* IV ("sit orator antequam dictor"), and the *postulare non desino* / *Ipsam rogo ut…* paragraphs read like *De Trinitate* I. The citation may therefore be **composite, or a misattribution carried over from Andrewes' own hand or from the 1675**. **I have not verified this against the text of Fulgentius, so it is recorded as a question, not a finding.** Kept exactly as printed in both layers, per the standing rule on references; **verify against Fulgentius (and Augustine) before print**, and if the tag is wrong, say so in a note rather than mending the line.

2. **⚠ `sit prius orator, quam doctor` turns on a pun that English cannot hold in one word.** *Orator* is both **one who prays** (from *orare*) and **a speaker**. The sentence means "let him be a pray-er before he is a teacher," and the joke is that the Latin word for *public speaker* is the word for *one who prays* — which is the whole argument of the paragraph: the preacher's power is *pietate orationum, quam orationis facultate*, by the piety of his **prayers** rather than the faculty of his **speech**, where *oratio* does double duty in exactly the same way. **The English must choose, and should choose praying**, flagging the loss rather than reaching for a strained "orator/orison" effect.

3. **`ut eructet, quod inde biberit, et effundat quod impleverit` — the drinking-and-pouring close.** Let him **belch out** what he has drunk there, and pour forth what has filled him. *Eructare* is the Vulgate's word at Ps. xlv. 1 (*Eructavit cor meum verbum bonum*) and carries no vulgarity in that register; the English should reach for the psalm's idiom ("my heart is inditing"/"poureth forth") rather than the physical verb, but must keep the sequence **drink first, then pour**, since that is the instruction.

4. **`elevet animam sitientem` before he *exserat proferentem linguam*** — let him lift up his **thirsting soul** before he puts out his **uttering tongue**. The two participles are matched and the order is the point; a translation that puts the tongue first has reversed the counsel.

5. **The three channels of teaching are a triad and stay one** — *sive per eloquia Scripturarum suarum, sive per sermocinationem fratrum, sive per inspirationis suæ internam suavioremque doctrinam*: by the **words of his Scriptures**, by the **conversation of the brethren**, by the **inward and sweeter teaching of his own inspiration**. The comparative *suavior* is doing real work (sweeter **than the other two**), and should not be flattened to "sweet."

6. **`præveniente ac subsequente misericordia` is the technical pair — PREVENIENT and following grace**, the language of the Articles and of the Collects ("Prevent us, O Lord, in all our doings"). Rendered with the received English terms, not paraphrased as "going before and coming after."

7. **The five-fold petition in the last sentence is a single sustained period and should stay one sentence if English can bear it**: teach me what I do not know · keep me in what I truly know · correct me where, as a man, I am deceived · confirm me where I stagger in the truth · deliver me from what is false and hurtful. Then the turn outward — *and make that to proceed from my mouth which is chiefly pleasing before the truth itself, so may it be acceptable to all the faithful.* The prayer moves from the preacher's own mind to his mouth to his hearers, in that order.

8. **⚠ `veritati … ab hac ipsissima veritate … Ipsam rogo` — the Truth is a PERSON here, and the English must let it be.** *Ipsam rogo* ("Her I ask", feminine, agreeing with *veritas*) is a prayer addressed to Truth, i.e. to Christ; the passage slides from truth-as-thing to Truth-as-addressee across three sentences. Capitalising the later instances is a legitimate way to carry it, but the shift must be visible, since a reader who misses it will think the author is praying to an abstraction.

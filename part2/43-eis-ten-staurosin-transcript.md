## Translator's flags

1. **⚠⚠ WRONG SORT AT PRINTED 392: `Πᾶς` is set where `Πῶς` is required** — in the poem's most famous line, *Πῶς, Θεὸς ὢν, ἔθανες!* ("How, being God, didst thou die!"). Verified at 900 dpi (`raw/end/c392-pos-429.jpg`): the vowel after Π is unmistakably **alpha** — a single bowl with a right-hand stroke — where the omega two words later (in `ὢν`) is the double-bowl glyph. The two are plainly different sorts in the same line, so this is not a reading-of-the-sort question like the upsilon trap; it is **alpha set for omega**. *Πᾶς* ("all, every", nominative masculine) is ungrammatical here and cannot be construed.
   **The facing Latin settles the sense**: printed 393 reads *Qui, Deus immortalis, mortuus es?* — *qui* as the old interrogative adverb, "**how**?" So the intended word is Πῶς.
   ⚠ **Unlike the Hebrew at printed 386, this leaf has NO duplicate scan** (392/393 were photographed only once, unlike 394/395 which were done three times), so the usual plate-vs-scan cross-check is unavailable. The judgement rests on two things instead: imaging noise does not turn a two-bowl omega into a clean one-bowl alpha carrying a well-formed circumflex, and the Latin independently gives "how." **Recorded as a compositor's wrong sort at high but not absolute confidence; kept as printed, and on the watch list for the non-Google digitization.**

2. **⚠ WRONG BREATHING AT PRINTED 394: `Ἡ` is set with a ROUGH breathing where the smooth is required.** The line is *Ἦ οὐχ ὁράας ὁλοπόρφυρον;* — the interrogative particle. Verified at 900 dpi (`raw/end/c394-eta-431.jpg`): the mark over the Η is a rough breathing (it matches the one over the omicron of *ὁράας* on the same line and is the mirror image of the smooth breathing on *οὐχ* beside it), and no accent is printed. **Ἡ with a rough breathing is the feminine definite article**, which cannot stand before οὐχ ὁράας. The facing Latin reads *Nonne cernis totum-purpuratum?* — "Dost thou not see…?" — so the particle is wanted. Kept as printed; a much lesser defect than flag 1, but the same class.

3. **⚠ THE FOUNT TRAP, twice.** The heading is set `ΕΙΣ ΤΗΝ ΤΟΤ ΧΡΙΣΤΟΤ ΣΤΑΤΡΩΣΙΝ` and printed 392 has `Τἱὸν ἔδωκε Πατήρ`. Per the §34 ruling these are **ΤΟΥ / ΧΡΙΣΤΟΥ / ΣΤΑΥΡΩΣΙΝ / Υἱὸν** — the flat-armed capital upsilon. Note the heading contains a **genuine** tau in ΤΗΝ, so the two glyphs sit side by side for comparison at 600 dpi (`raw/end/c392a-429.jpg`), which is the clearest confirmation of the trap in the volume so far. Transcribed Υ as a reading of the sort, not an emendation.

4. **⚠ WORD-SPLITS ACROSS LINE-ENDS ARE THE POET'S, NOT THE COMPOSITOR'S, and are kept.** Twice on printed 394 a word breaks across the line: *Σιδονίης ἁλὸς, **ἀλ-** / **λ'** αἵματι σταζομένῳ* and *Ἑὸν αἷμα **χέαν-** / **τ'.** ὀλίγ' ἄττα λείβειν δάκρυα*. These are **not** the turned-line hyphens of a justified prose measure — the lines are far short of the measure, and the Latin recto reproduces the same lineation without splitting its own words. They are metrical, and the effect (a word torn in half across the break, at *blood* and at *poured out*) is deliberate. **Do not close them up**, and note that `transcript2tex.py` must not treat them as justification hyphens.

5. **⚠ THE INDENTATION HAS MORE LEVELS HERE THAN ANYWHERE ELSE IN THE VOLUME, and it is approximated.** The plate steps the verse in at least four distinct depths, and some steps are finer than a full quad. The transcript normalises to **0 / 4 / 8 / 12 / 16 spaces** and the Latin recto is given the *same* normalised depths, since the plate keeps the two columns in step. **The approximation is disclosed here rather than hidden**: at typesetting time the depths should be re-measured off the plate, because §42's rule holds and is sharper in verse — the mirror is read across the spread, so a depth changed on one side breaks the parallel on the other.

6. **`Μακάρεσσί τε δαίμοσι` — δαίμοσι here means the ANGELS, and the Latin says so.** Printed 393 renders it *Beatisque Angelis*. Andrewes is writing in a deliberately Homeric-classicising register (*βροτοῖσι*, *Μακάρεσσι*, *Νηλεέσσι*, *Πικρῇσι συμπλοκῇσι*, the uncontracted *ὁράας*, *πρῆϋ κάρηνον*), and in that register δαίμονες are the blessed spirits, not demons. **This is the section's worst translation trap**: "demons" would be a serious and pious-looking error, and the Latin is the guard against it.

7. **`ὑπ' ἐχθρῶν` (Greek) against `pro hostibus` (Latin) — a real divergence, and the Greek governs.** The Greek says the Son was given as a ransom **at the hands of / by** enemies; the Latin says **for** enemies, which is the familiar theology (Rom. v. 10). *ὑπέρ* does not elide to *ὑπ'*, so the Greek cannot simply be read as the Latin. **The English follows the Greek** per CONVENTIONS §6 and records the Latin's reading here; this is a crux and the English does not pick a side by smoothing it.

8. **Three more places where the Latin construes rather than translates**, all kept and all noted: (a) *ἄπληστε* ("insatiate, never filled") is rendered ***vaste***, "vast" — the Latin has taken a word about *never being satisfied* and made it a word about *size*; (b) *Τί **Σοι**, τί πρῶτον εἴπω;* — the Greek's dative is "what shall I say **to thee**", the Latin's *Quid **tuum*** is "what **of thine**"; (c) *Θέσφατον θρίαμβον* ("the divinely-uttered triumph", the θέσφατα being oracles) becomes ***Mysticum*** *triumphum*. In each case the English follows the Greek.

9. **`Κρανίων ὄρος` = the hill of Skulls, i.e. Calvary**, and the Latin makes it explicit (*Calvariorum mons*). The Greek keeps the common noun; the English keeps it too, because *ἀμφιβρέμει* — "roars round about me" — needs a hill and not a place-name to work.

10. **`ὀλλύντων, ὀλλυμένων τε` — the same verb active and passive, side by side**, Homeric participles: *of them that destroy and them that perish*. The Latin catches it exactly (*Perdentium, pereuntiumque*). Any English that varies the two verbs loses the point, which is that the noise on the hill is one noise made by both parties.

11. **`Πᾶς/Πῶς, Θεὸς ὢν, ἔθανες!` ends in an EXCLAMATION in the Greek and a QUESTION MARK in the Latin** (*mortuus es?*). Kept as printed on both sides. The difference is worth preserving: the Greek is a cry, the Latin an interrogation.

12. **No `[?]` in this section.** All four leaves are clean apart from the two wrong sorts at flags 1–2; the Greek was read at 600 dpi on six crops (`c392a`, `c392b`, `c392c`, `c394a`, `c394b`, `c394c`) and at 900 dpi on two (`c392-pos`, `c394-eta`).

# Part II — Preces Quotidianæ — §43 On the Crucifying of Christ, Monostrophics — English

Translated from the **Greek** of `43-eis-ten-staurosin-transcript.md` (printed 392, 394), line-keyed page by page; the Latin recto consulted as witness and its divergences flagged, not blended. Register per CONVENTIONS §8–10. **Parity 2/2** (two Greek pages), per the mirror convention established at §42.
**⚠ This is the last section of PART II.** The poem ends at printed 395; PARS TERTIA begins overleaf.
**⚠ Rendered as line-keyed plain English, NOT as English verse — see flag 1.**

<!-- printed 392 — English of the Greek page -->

        ON THE CRUCIFYING OF CHRIST
                Monostrophics.

    ENOUGH of inspired babbling;
    Change, O Muse, thy strings,
    and taking up the lyre,
    strike a solemn hymn
    to the King upon his cross.
    O Treasure of wonders, unsated
    and unbounded:
        What shall I say to thee, and what first?
I would tell a thing unspeakable to mortals,
    and a riddle to the blessed spirits,
            How, being God, thou didst die!
I would tell the untrackable deeps
    of Mercy, that for a ransom, at the hands of enemies,
            the Father gave the Son.
            I would cry aloud on high
            the oracled triumph
            of the three-days' dead,
            and Hades led captive,
            and death subdued by death.
But the hill of Skulls roars round about me,
and mine ears are full of a many-voiced clamour,
            of them that destroy, and them that perish.
    Leap forth to the hill, mine eyes;
    who is it hangeth in the midst of three,
    (how nothing like unto the two!)

<!-- printed 394 — English of the Greek page -->

    and is stretched out upon the four-yoked stock?
            his gentle head
            sweetly bowed down,
and the holy strength of his arms
spread abroad athwart,
                with pitiless nails
pierced through on this side and on that!
Wretched man, dost thou look on this unmoved?
            Wail sore,
            rend thy garment,
            and smite thy breast,
            and tear thy hair,
            and stir thy bowels.
Dost thou not see him all in purple?
            gleaming not with the flame
            of the Sidonian sea, but
    with blood dripping down,
            the one part from his temples
            girt about with the thorn's
            sharp-mouthed shearing;
            the other from his limbs
            scored with the scourge's
            bitter embracings.
            Open, open
            the gates of thine eyes,
            and the springs of thine eyelids
loose:   drizzle down, wet the earth,
            with him that unsparingly
            poureth out his own blood.
    To shed some few tears —
            what grudging is that, O mortal?

## Translator's flags

1. **⚠ THE RENDERING DECISION, made once and recorded: line-keyed plain English, not English verse — and the 1853 itself decided it.** This is the only metrical Greek in the volume, and the obvious temptation is to answer verse with verse. **The edition's own Latin does not.** Printed 393 and 395 give *Satis fuit inspirationis nugatoriæ; / Musa, permuta chordas…* — which is not verse, scans as nothing, and tracks the Greek **line for line and indent for indent**. So the 1853's own facing column is a line-keyed plain crib, and matching it is both the faithful choice and the only one compatible with CONVENTIONS §7 and with the Prototype B mirror, where a spread that will not page-fit flips the whole verso/recto parallel. **An English verse rendering would break the line-keying, break the page-fit, and set this edition against its own base text.** The loss is real and is declared here: the poem's metre, and its Homeric colouring, survive only in the diction.

2. **⚠ The English of printed 392 reads `How, being God, thou didst die!` — and the plate reads `Πᾶς`, not `Πῶς`.** See transcript flag 1. The Greek as set cannot be construed (*πᾶς* is "all, every"), and the facing Latin gives *Qui, Deus immortalis, mortuus es?* — "how?" **The English therefore gives the sense the Latin confirms, and the transcript keeps the wrong sort**, exactly as with the garbled Hebrew at printed 386. Unlike that case there is **no duplicate scan** of this leaf to cross-check, so the correction is recorded at high but not absolute confidence and goes on the pre-print list. This is the line the whole poem exists for, so it must not be quietly patched in either direction.

3. **⚠ `Μακάρεσσί τε δαίμοσι` = "the blessed spirits," i.e. THE ANGELS — never "demons."** The Latin says *Beatisque Angelis*, and the poem is written throughout in a deliberately Homeric register (*βροτοῖσι* for men, *Μακάρεσσι* for the blessed, *Νηλεέσσι*, *Πικρῇσι συμπλοκῇσι*, uncontracted *ὁράας*, *πρῆϋ κάρηνον*), in which δαίμονες are the good spirits. **"A riddle to the demons" would be smooth, pious-sounding, and badly wrong.** I have used "the blessed spirits" rather than "the angels" so that the classicising word is still audible, with the Latin's gloss recorded here.

4. **⚠ `at the hands of enemies` follows the GREEK against the Latin, and the crux is left open.** The Greek is *λύτρον **ὑπ' ἐχθρῶν***, a ransom **by/at the hands of** enemies; the Latin recto reads *lytrum **pro hostibus***, a ransom **for** enemies — which is the familiar theology (Rom. v. 10) and is almost certainly what a reader expects. But *ὑπέρ* does not elide to *ὑπ'*, so the Greek will not yield the Latin's sense, and CONVENTIONS §6 gives the Greek the text. **The English follows the Greek and does not smooth toward the Latin**; the divergence is recorded rather than resolved.

5. **`the oracled triumph` for `Θέσφατον θρίαμβον`.** *Θέσφατος* is what is *spoken by God* — the θέσφατα are oracles — and the Latin's *Mysticum* has already softened it to "mystical." The English keeps the speaking: the triumph of the resurrection is a thing **uttered by God**, not a mystery in the modern sense. "Oracled" is strained English and is chosen deliberately over "mystical," which would silently adopt the Latin's construal.

6. **`O Treasure of wonders, unsated and unbounded` — the Latin has taken `ἄπληστε` the wrong way and the English does not follow it.** *Ἄπληστος* is "insatiate, never filled"; the Latin renders it *vaste*, "vast," turning a word about appetite into a word about size. Kept as "unsated," which is what the Greek says of the Treasure — it is never used up. The Latin's reading is recorded in the transcript.

7. **`What shall I say to thee` follows the Greek dative `Σοι`**, against the Latin's *Quid **tuum*** ("what of thine"). A small divergence, but it changes who the poem is talking to at its opening: in the Greek the poet turns and addresses Christ directly, and everything after that is spoken to him.

8. **`of them that destroy, and them that perish` keeps ONE verb in two voices.** *Ὀλλύντων, ὀλλυμένων τε* is the same Homeric verb active and passive, and the Latin catches it exactly (*Perdentium, pereuntiumque*). Varying the English verbs — "the killers and the dying" — would lose the point, which is that the roar on the hill is a single noise made by both parties at once.

9. **`the four-yoked stock` for `τετράζυγι πρέμνῳ`.** *Πρέμνον* is the stump or trunk of a tree, not a "cross," and *τετράζυγος* is "four-yoked" — the cross described as a piece of timber with four arms, by a poet refusing the word everyone expects. The Latin agrees (*quadrijugo stipite*). **"Four-armed cross" would be clearer and would throw the effect away.**

10. **The imperatives at 394 are kept short and bare** — *wail · rend · smite · tear · stir* — because the Greek is five clipped commands in five short lines and the Latin matches them one for one. English wants to pad these ("weep bitterly," "rend thy garments in pieces"); the padding would ruin the acceleration into *Dost thou not see him all in purple?*

11. **`the Sidonian sea` = the murex, i.e. Tyrian purple**, and the Latin makes it explicit (*Tyrii maris*). The Greek keeps the periphrasis and so does the English, because the sentence turns on the contrast between a dye and blood, and naming the dye-trade too plainly deadens it.

12. **`Dost thou not see him all in purple?` — and the plate's particle is wrong.** The Greek is set `Ἡ` with a **rough** breathing (the feminine article) where the interrogative `Ἦ` is required; see transcript flag 2. The Latin's *Nonne cernis* settles the sense. Kept as printed in the transcript, given its sense here.

13. **The word-splits are NOT reproduced in the English, and this is a deliberate departure disclosed on the page.** At 394 the Greek tears two words across the line-break — *ἀλ- / λ'* and *χέαν- / τ'* — at "but [with blood]" and at "poured out." English cannot split a word at a line-end without looking like a typographic error rather than a metrical effect, so the English lines are unbroken while the transcript keeps the splits. **This is the one place in the section where the English is not strictly line-for-line**, and a note in the finished book should say so.

14. **`what grudging is that, O mortal?`** for *Τίς φθόνος, ὦ βροτέ;* — *φθόνος* is grudging, stinting, begrudging a thing to someone. The whole poem ends on the accusation that the reader is **being stingy with his tears** while Christ is unstinting (*ἀφειδῶς*) with his blood; the two words answer each other across four lines and the English keeps both. The Latin's *Quænam invidia sit* is the same charge.

## Translator's flags

1. **The two Greek lines carry no references in the 1853, and both are LXX.** Line 1 = **Gen. xxiv. 12** (*εὐόδωσον ἐναντίον ἐμοῦ σήμερον* — Abraham's servant at the well, "send me good speed this day"), which is why the Latin immediately below invokes that same servant's journey. Line 2 = **Exod. xxxiii. 15** (Moses: "If thy presence go not with me, carry us not up hence"). The 1853 prints *Ἐὰν μή* where the LXX has *Εἰ μή*; **kept as printed**. Supply both references in brackets in the English — the section is unintelligible without them, and the pairing (servant's journey, Moses' refusal to move without the Presence) is the prayer's whole argument.

2. **The Latin relative clauses are gapped — the verb is never supplied.** *Qui puerorum Abrahæ ductu Angeli, / Magorum iter [ductu] Stellæ; / Qui Petrum fluctuantem, / Paulum naufragantem;* — four objects, no governing verb, resolving only at *Adesto mihi, Domine*. The English must keep the suspension (a bracketed `[didst guide]` / `[didst uphold]` is the least intrusive fix) and must not close each clause into a sentence.

3. **`Magorum iter | Stellæ` is a printed two-column echo**, not a continuous line: *Stellæ* stands off to the right, under *ductu Angeli* on the line above, so the two guidances are set against each other (an Angel led the servant, a Star the Magi). Rendered ` | `.

4. **`Conduc, et deduc, et reduc` is a three-fold pun on *ducere* and must stay one.** *Conduc* — lead me along with thee; *deduc* — lead me out and onward; *reduc* — lead me back home. A journey has three parts and the Latin names them in three prefixes on one root. **Do not translate all three as "lead"** without keeping the going-out and the coming-home audible; this is the line the prayer exists for.

5. **`Exsurgat Deus, et dissipentur inimici` (Ps. lxviii. 1)** is the psalm of the Ark setting forward (Num. x. 35, "Rise up, Lord, and let thine enemies be scattered") — a travelling verse, which is why it is here. Use the Coverdale/BCP form (§9).

6. **`Discedite a me, maligni; Scrutabor mandata Dei mei` (Ps. cxix. 115)** — Coverdale "Away from me, ye wicked: I will keep the commandments of my God." Kept; *scrutabor* is stronger than "keep" (I will search out), but Coverdale governs the psalm-line per §9 and the divergence is noted here.

7. **⚠ Both columns of the Works of Mercy are medieval MNEMONIC VERSES quoted verbatim, which is why their moods disagree.** *Visito, poto, cibo, redimo, tego, colligo, condo* is the standard hexameter for the seven corporal works (**first-person indicative** throughout: I visit, I give drink, I feed, I ransom, I clothe, I harbour, I bury). The spiritual column — *Consule, plecte, doce, solare, remitte, fer, ora* — is the companion verse in the **imperative** (counsel, chastise, teach, comfort, forgive, bear, pray). **The mixed mood is not a slip and must not be regularised**: Andrewes is copying two received memory-verses, not composing a matched pair. Keep both moods in English, and keep both columns as terse as the Latin — these are pegs for memory, not sentences.

8. **The corporal seven are the Matt. xxv. 35–36 list plus burial** (*condo*), the traditional seventh drawn from Tobit; *colligo* is "I take in / harbour" the stranger (*hospes eram et collegistis me*), not "I gather." *Tego* covers both clothing and sheltering; the mnemonic's economy is the point and the English should not expand it.

            THE MORNING HYMN.*

GLORY be to God on high,
    and on earth peace,
    good will among men.
We praise thee,
    we bless thee,
    we worship thee,
    we glorify thee,
    we give thanks to thee,
    for thy great glory,
O Lord, heavenly King,
    O God the Father Almighty,
    O Lord, the only-begotten Son,
    Jesu Christ,
    and O Holy Ghost.
O Lord God,
    the Lamb of God,
    the Son of the Father,
Thou that takest away the sins of the world,
    have mercy upon us.
Thou that takest away the sins of the world,
    receive our prayer.
Thou that sittest at the right hand of the Father,
    have mercy upon us.

    * This is perhaps that Hymn which C. Pliny makes mention of, in the
Epistle xxvii. of book x., in these words: "That the Christians were wont on
a set day to meet before daylight, and to sing a Hymn to Christ as unto God,
by turns among themselves."

            THE EVENING HYMN.

O GLADSOME light of the holy glory
        of the immortal Father, heavenly, holy,
    blessed, O Jesu Christ,
we, being come to the setting of the sun,
    beholding the evening light,
        praise in hymns the Father,
            and the Son,
        and the Holy Spirit of God.
Worthy art thou at all times
    to be hymned with holy voices,
        O Son of God,
            thou giver of life.
Wherefore the world doth glorify thee.

## Translator's flags

1. **⚠ The asterisk rows are kept in the English, and they mean something the English reader cannot otherwise see.** Eight asterisks stand in each column at the point where **the facing column carries a clause this one lacks** — at 389 (Latin) because the Greek has *and O Holy Ghost*, and at 390 (Greek, reproduced above) because the Latin has *cum Sancto Spiritu*. Since the English translates the Greek, it inherits the Greek's row of asterisks and **not** the Latin's clause: the English of printed 390 must not read "with the Holy Ghost, in the glory of God the Father," however familiar that is, because the Greek does not say it and the asterisks are the 1853 saying so. A note in the finished book should explain the device once; the text itself keeps the marks. See transcript flag 2.

2. **⚠ `and O Holy Ghost` (printed 388) is the Alexandrine reading and is the most striking thing in the section.** The Greek Gloria's roll of vocatives runs *O Lord, heavenly King · O God the Father Almighty · O Lord, the only-begotten Son · Jesu Christ · **and O Holy Ghost*** — the address opens out to the Third Person where the Western Gloria (and the Prayer Book) do not. It is translated plainly and left standing, as §29's prayer for the departed and §38's petition to die rather than offend again were. The Latin's asterisks on the facing page are the edition's own acknowledgement that the two texts differ here.

3. **The Prayer Book is the register but NOT the text, and the places where they part are all noted.** Where the Greek and the BCP coincide, the BCP's wording is used without apology, because the reader has said these words all his life and this edition is for praying. Where the Greek departs, the Greek governs. The departures: **(a)** *good will **among** men* — the Greek εὐδοκία is nominative, so the good will is among men, not a quality of the men who receive peace (and the 1853's Latin *bonæ voluntates* agrees with the Greek against the Vulgate); **(b)** *O Lord, heavenly King* — the Greek has no "God" there; **(c)** *and O Holy Ghost* — see flag 2; **(d)** *thou only art Lord, **Jesus Christ*** — the Greek is nominative, not the BCP's vocative "thou only, O Christ"; **(e)** *unto the glory of God the Father* — the Greek has no "art most high," and here the Latin agrees with the Greek, so it is the BCP that carries the extra.

4. **Five verbs of praise are kept as five.** *We praise · we bless · we worship · we glorify · we give thanks* renders Αἰνοῦμεν / εὐλογοῦμεν / προσκυνοῦμεν / δοξολογοῦμεν / εὐχαριστοῦμεν. The BCP's habit is to run four of these together; the fifth Greek verb, **δοξολογοῦμεν**, is a distinct word (to speak glory, to doxologise) and is not to be collapsed into δοξάζω's "glorify" by dropping a line. English cannot easily distinguish the two glory-verbs in one word each, and I have not strained for it; the loss is recorded rather than papered over.

5. **`Thou that takest away` and `Thou that sittest` keep the BCP's participial address**, which is also exactly the Greek's (Ὁ αἴρων, Ὁ καθήμενος — articular participles used as vocatives). This is one of the places where the received English is not a compromise but the right translation.

6. **`πρόσδεξαι τὴν δέησιν ἡμῶν` — "receive our prayer."** *Πρόσδεξαι* is *receive unto thyself*, and the BCP's "receive our prayer" is exact. The Latin's *suscipe deprecationem nostram* is the same act; no divergence to flag.

7. **The footnote is the 1853's CONJECTURE and is translated as one.** *Hoc **forsitan** Carmen illud est* — "This is **perhaps** that Hymn." The hedge is the editor's and is kept; without it the note asserts that the Gloria in excelsis is the very hymn Pliny's informants described, which no one can know. **The reference `Epistola xxvii. lib. x.` is wrong and is translated as printed** — the Christians letter is Pliny **x. 96** (97 in some editions), and *xxvii* is almost certainly a misprint for *xcvii*. Flagged, not corrected, per the standing rule. Note also that the note is **editorial apparatus**, like §41's closing note, and must be set as such in print.

8. **`O GLADSOME light` — Keble is a crib and is not copied.** John Keble's "Hail, gladdening Light" (1834) is the famous English Φῶς ἱλαρόν and was consulted after drafting, per CONVENTIONS §12; his metrical paraphrase rearranges the syntax freely and cannot be imported into a line-keyed edition anyway. *Gladsome* is kept because it is the right word for ἱλαρός — cheerful, kindly, the opposite of harsh light — and predates Keble in English; the rest of the rendering follows the Greek's own order, which is the whole point of the line-keying.

9. **The Φῶς ἱλαρόν's grammar is one long suspended sentence and stays one.** *Having come … having seen … we praise in hymns* — two aorist participles before the main verb, and the English keeps the suspension ("we, being come to the setting of the sun, beholding the evening light, praise in hymns…") rather than breaking it into three sentences. The hymn's whole effect is that nothing is said until the lamps are lit.

10. **`Ἄξιος εἶ` is second person and is rendered so** — "Worthy art thou at all times to be hymned," not the received text's impersonal "It is meet at all times that thou shouldest be hymned." Both columns of the 1853 carry the second person (*Dignus es*), so this is the recension and not a slip. It makes the hymn more direct than the version most readers know, and that difference is the reason to keep it.

11. **`ζωὴν ὁ διδούς` — "thou giver of life," a title.** The article makes it the credal epithet (the Life-giver, τὸ ζωοποιόν), not a relative clause; the Latin's bare *vitæ dator* confirms it. "Who givest life" would be smoother and would turn a name into a description.

12. **`Wherefore the world doth glorify thee`** for Διὸ ὁ κόσμος σε δοξάζει — *the world*, not "all the world" and not "creation." The line answers *Worthy art thou*, and the logic is that because he is worthy at all times, the world is at this moment doing it.

## Translator's flags

1. **The two pieces answer each other and are filed together.** The *Oratio præparatoria ante* Εὐλογίαν closes what §40's third catena opened (Occasions before the Eucharist-or-Blessing → the prayer itself), and the *Monita* then turns the whole penitential sequence into a **practice**: a nightly rule with a stated duration and a stated result. The section is the hinge out of Part II's penitential matter and into the hymns.

2. **⚠ THE HEBREW AT 386 IS GARBLED IN THE PLATE, and the English discloses the repair rather than hiding it.** As set, the second word is **חזתלת**, which is not a Hebrew word; what the sentence needs is **תוחלת** (*tocheleth*), "hope" — and Andrewes' own Latin on the next page proves it, *Ecce **spes** fructus et salutis*. The mechanism is a compositor's: **two sorts transposed (ת ↔ ח) and a zayin set for a vav**. Confirmed a **plate defect, not a scan artifact** — the duplicate scan at PDF 423 reads the same five letters. **The transcript keeps the garble; the English prints the plate's reading, then the corrected reading, then the sense** — three things on one line, because a reader who cannot read Hebrew must still be able to see that a repair has been made and what it was. Silently printing the good Hebrew would be an emendation of the kind this edition does not make. **Goes on the watch list for restoration before print.** ⚠ **CORRECTED 2026-08-04: Brightman cannot settle this from his text.** He marks Hebrew by printing its translation in *italic*, and **italics do not survive OCR** — so the question needs **his page images**, not his text, and in any case he prints no Hebrew of his own. The Harley MS or a 600-dpi read of the plate remain the real routes.

3. **`Rab. J.` is left abbreviated and unexpanded**, as the 1853 leaves it. The saying — do not defer repentance to the morrow, for the hope of your gain and your rescue will prove false if you die this night — is the commonplace of the rabbinic *teshuvah* literature (R. Jonah Gerondi's *Sha'arei Teshuvah* is the likeliest source), but the edition does not say and neither does the English. On the pre-print list beside §39's Fulgentius.

4. **⚠ `Χωρῆσαι εἰς μετάνοιαν καὶ εὐλογίας` is a same-line gloss and is rendered ONCE, not twice** — per the ruling recorded at transcript flag 7. *Vacare* (to be at leisure for) and *χωρῆσαι* (to have room for) are the same thought in two languages, and the shared idea is **emptiness made ready**: he is asking to be cleared out enough to hold repentance and blessing. The English folds the two into one sentence, keeps the Greek visible, and lets "leisure … room enough" carry both verbs. Note the plural **εὐλογίας** against the heading's singular; not levelled.

5. **The trumpet figure is military and must stay military.** ἐγκελευστικός is the **charge** — literally the call that urges on, *ad agendum accommodatus*, fitted for the doing; ἀνακλητικός is the **recall**, the signal that brings the scattered back in. Andrewes' application depends on the pair: the morning rouses like a charge, **the evening examination is a recall** — and *palantes*, the stragglers who have wandered off from the line, is what he thinks the day has made of him. "Stragglers" is deliberate; "wanderers" loses the army. Note the 1853 latinises the second word in the application (*quasi anacletico*), and the English keeps that as an English word too ("as by a recall") rather than reaching back to the Greek.

6. **`ad se ducemque suum` — "unto itself and unto its own captain."** *Dux* here is the army-word the figure has just set up, so the mind is called back to its **commander**, not its "leader" or "guide." Whether the captain is God or the reason is left open by the Latin and is left open in the English.

7. **The two renderings of Jer. xvii. 9 are BOTH kept and are deliberately different.** The Greek line is "the heart is deep beyond all things" (LXX); the Latin adds *et tortuosum*, crooked. **They are not a doublet** (contrast §27) — Andrewes is stacking two readings, and the third line, "the old man is covered over with a thousand wrappings," is the conclusion drawn from both: deep, so you cannot see the bottom; crooked, so you cannot see straight down it. Conforming the two English lines would destroy the argument.

8. **`πρόσεχε σεαυτῷ` / `Attende tibi ipsi` is rendered once** — same-line gloss, per flag 4 and transcript flag 7. "Take heed unto thyself" is the received English of the phrase (Deut. iv. 9, Acts xx. 28, and the title of Basil's homily), and the Greek is kept visible beside it.

9. **⚠ The `Quid hodie` brace is an OUTLINE and stays one — no line is made into a sentence.** The English keeps all four levels and the ` | ` columns of the transcript. Two things the reader must be able to see: (a) the four acts are **doing, saying, reading, writing** — a bishop's day, and the pairing of the second column with the first is the point (what you *read* and *wrote* is examined exactly as what you *did* and *said*); (b) the four tests below apply to **all four acts at once**, which is what the tall brace means. *Deceat* is "beseem" — the test is fitness to a station (Christian, priest, father), not moral quality in the abstract.

10. **`operetur salutem { tuam, / aliorum. }` — "salvation," not "welfare."** *Salus* can be either, and the schema is practical enough that "good" would read naturally; but the whole *Monita* ends in *animam tuam … eripueris* and *perfectus virtutis habitus*, so salvation is meant. The pairing **thine own / others'** is kept in that order as printed.

11. **`καὶ ἔσκαλλε τὸ Πνεῦμα` — "dug over his spirit," and the spade is kept.** LXX Ps. lxxvi. 7 (Ps. lxxvii. 6 in English Bibles); *σκάλλω* is to hoe or grub up, and both Coverdale and the AV reach for "made diligent search." Andrewes puts it in the **third person about David** and drops the *μου*, so it is bare "the spirit." "Searched his soul" is the one rendering to avoid — it is a dead modern idiom and the whole line exists for the image of a man turning himself over like ground.

12. **The Areopagus is kept as a proper name.** *In hoc Areopagitico et nocturno examine* — the Areopagus was Athens' homicide court, which sat at night and in darkness so that the judges saw only the case. Hence "not the advocate of thy sins, but their judge," and "at the bar of thine own mind." **The court words are kept throughout** (advocate, judge, bar, sentence), continuous with §7's *Allegatio* and §32's *Interpellatio*; "be honest with yourself" would be the same counsel with the architecture knocked out.

13. **`O quis dabit menti meæ flagella, Quæ peccatis meis non parcant?`** — Ecclus. xxiii. 2 with Andrewes' own addition. The Vulgate/Douay have scourges over the *thought*; he has them over the **mind**, and adds the relative clause *that shall not spare my sins*. The addition is his and is the sharpest thing on the page; it is translated as a full relative clause and not softened to "unsparing scourges."

14. **The Ausonius couplet is VERSE and is set as two lines with some metrical shape** — the only classical verse in Part II. *Nec prius … quam* is "not before … until," and the English keeps the suspension ("Nor let thine eyes sink first … / Ere thou have told again …") rather than untangling it into prose order. *Longi diei* does its complaining by the adjective, and "the long day's deeds" keeps that.

15. **God, Cato, Pythagoras and David stand side by side and are not sorted.** The page argues from the six days of creation (*et vidit quod bona essent*), from a Roman Stoic's daily audit, from a Greek philosopher's verses, and from the Psalms — **pagan and scriptural without apology or hierarchy**, which is characteristic of Andrewes and of the *Monita*'s whole method. The English does not add "even" or "likewise the heathen" or any other softener.

16. **`rationem exigebat` is an ACCOUNTING word — "required of himself an account."** Cato audits the day as a steward audits a estate; it pairs with God's *recognitio operum*, the review of the works. Keep the ledger sense; "reflected on his day" is the wrong register in both directions.

17. **The closing note is the EDITOR'S and is translated as apparatus.** *Rev. Usserius … Lib. de Symbolis* = **Ussher, *De Romanæ Ecclesiæ Symbolo Apostolico***, and the *MS. Alexandrinum Bibliothecæ Regiæ* is the **Codex Alexandrinus** in the King's Library, whose appendix of canticles is the source of the two hymns that follow. The English is given so that a reader of the finished book knows what the note says, but it **must be set as apparatus — small, editorial, and outside the devotional text**. It is not Andrewes praying.

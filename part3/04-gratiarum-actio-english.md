## Translator's flags

**Parity 6/6**, line-exact on every page.

1. **⚠ The volume does not end here, and the map said it did.** Printed 430 closes this section with two-thirds of the page blank, which reads exactly like the end of a book — but printed **431 opens MATUTINÆ PRECES** and **433 VESPERTINÆ PRECES**, and the last words of the *Preces Privatæ* are at printed **436**. STRUCTURE's "~430" was six pages short, the same class of error as its "~397" for the end of Part II. **The standing lesson — read one page past — has now paid for itself at both ends of the volume.**

2. **The section's architecture, and why it must not be smoothed.** It is a thanksgiving in three movements that change **grammatical kind**, not just subject: first a **catena of Benedictus-texts** in whole sentences (425–428, ending in the Revelation doxology); then the **`Quod` brace** (428) — thirteen bare participles and adjectives, no verb, hanging off *that I am*; then the **`Pro` braces** (429–430) — five stacked lists of what he gives thanks *for*, still with no main verb, until *Confiteor Tibi et confitebor* at last supplies one on the final page. **The whole of 428–430 is one suspended sentence**, and the English keeps it suspended: no "I thank Thee for" is inserted at the head of the braces, because the delay is the design.

3. **`Quod { Sum, Vivo, … Literatus; }` (428) is §24's estate-catalogue again** — the fourth recension-parallel in Part III (after §1↔§23+§38, §2↔§17, and §3's echoes). §24 (printed 345–346) ran the same reckoning as a series of antitheses — *a living soul and not a senseless thing, a man and not a brute, free-born and not a* verna, *lettered and not a mechanic* — and closed on *Donatus donis { Gratiæ / Naturæ / Fortunæ }*. Here the antitheses are stripped away and only the positive members are left, and **the three-fold *Gratiæ / Naturæ / Fortunæ* turns up on the very next page** as *Pro bonis { naturæ, / fortunæ, / gratiæ; }*. Same reckoning, differently set. **Neither conformed to the other**; the shared English words (*free-born*, *lettered*) are the same in both because the Latin is.

4. **`Civilis` (428) → "A citizen," not "civil."** In the estate-catalogue the word names a condition, not a manner: born into a commonwealth rather than among barbarians — the rung between *endued with reason* and *a Christian*. **`Ingenuus`** is **"free-born"** as at §24, distinct from *Liber*, "free": one is birth, the other is condition, and the plate sets them on the same line without punctuation between, which is kept.

5. **`{ Mentis, / Sensuum, / Membrorum, } compos;` (428) → "in possession { of mind, / of senses, / of members }."** *Compos mentis* is the phrase English borrowed whole; extending it to senses and members is Andrewes'. "Sound of mind, sense and limb" would be idiomatic and would lose the legal *compos*, which is what makes the three-fold extension a joke worth making.

6. **`Catechismo` (429) → "Catechizing," not "the Catechism."** The list is of things done to him — redemption, regeneration, catechizing, calling — and a bishop giving thanks for his own catechizing means the teaching he received, not the text. **`Præventione`** is **"Prevention"** in the formularies' sense (prevenient grace, as §39 and §3 both ruled), and stands next to *Curatione*, "Healing," in a pair of what grace does before and after.

7. **`Si quid bene gerimus` (429) → "If we do anything well."** The one conditional in a list of nouns, and the hesitation is the point — he will not name his good works as a benefit received without hedging that there may be none. Kept as a clause, not flattened into "our good deeds."

8. **`Injuriis` in the list of what profited him (430) is the sting of the section.** Writings, sermons, discourse, prayers, examples, **reproof, injuries** — the last two are what other people did *against* him, set in the same brace as their prayers and thanked for in the same breath. **"Injuries," not "wrongs"** — the older English keeps the legal edge and the New Testament's *injuriosus*.

9. **The two facing brace-columns at 430** — *known / manifest / which we remember* **or** *unknown / hidden / which we forget*; *I bless / I give thanks* **and** *I will bless / I will give thanks*. The first pair is the sweep (nothing left out on either side), the second the tense (present and future together). English keeps the rows and the middle cue, and keeps *confess* twice in *Confiteor Tibi et confitebor* — **"I confess unto Thee and will confess"** — because the doubling is the same figure one line early.

10. **`Anima quæ benedixit, impinguabitur` (425) → "The soul that hath blessed shall be made fat."** Prov. xi. 25 (the plate says x. 25 — transcription note 3). The AV has "The liberal soul shall be made fat," reading the Hebrew as generosity; the Vulgate's *benedixit* reads it as blessing, and Andrewes needs the blessing, since it is the text he opens a thanksgiving with. **The Latin governs, and the AV's reading is not imported.** "Made fat" is kept — it is the AV's own phrase for the second half of the verse, and softening it to "enriched" would lose a deliberately physical word in a section that goes on to give thanks for skin, flesh, bones and sinews.

11. **⚠ `Cantemus Domino Isaac et Israelitarum` (425) is rendered exactly as it stands, and the English does not repair it.** The song at the Red Sea is Moses' and Israel's; **Isaac has no part in it**, and no construal makes him fit. The line is translated straight — *Let us sing unto the Lord of Isaac and of the Israelites* — and the difficulty is left visible rather than mended into "the God of Isaac" or "of Israel." **On the pre-print verification list** (transcription note 3).

12. **Coverdale governs the Psalter throughout, and at 427 it governs a whole page.** *Praise the Lord, O my soul, and all that is within me praise His holy Name* · *forget not all His benefits* · *thy youth is renewed like the eagle's* · *Thou hast turned my heaviness into joy: Thou hast put off my sackcloth, and girded me with gladness* — all Coverdale, all matching Andrewes' Latin line for line, because both come through the Vulgate. The one place the crib is set aside is **`et non compungar`** (427): the BCP has "that I may sing of thy praise without ceasing," where the Latin says *and not be pricked* — a different verse entirely — so the Latin is followed and the divergence noted here.

13. **`in factura Tua` (426) → "through Thy work."** Ps. 92:4, and Coverdale's "For thou, Lord, hast made me glad through thy works" is followed, with the singular kept singular: *factura* is the thing made — himself — which is what the whole page has been about (fashioned, milked out, curdled, clothed with skin and flesh). The second half of the verse then moves to the plural *operibus manuum Tuarum*, and the shift is Andrewes' and is kept.

## Collation findings — Brightman 1903 + recension parallels (2026-08-04)

> Applied to the ENGLISH layer only. **No transcript is altered**; the 1853 plate stands as printed. Method and full ledger: `apparatus/BRIGHTMAN-collation.md`.

- **✅✅ THE `Isaac` CRUX AT PRINTED 425 IS SOLVED — the word is `Mosis`.** The flags recorded that *Isaac* "has no part in the song at the Red Sea and no construal makes him fit," translated it straight and left the difficulty visible. Brightman shows the catena **tags each text with whose blessing it is**: *"Blessed be the Lord **(of Jethro)** — Ex. xviii 10"*, then *"I will sing unto the Lord **(of Moses and the Israelites)** — Ex. xv 1."* So the line is **`Cantemus Domino Mosis et Israelitarum`**, Exod. xv. **1** — *"Then sang Moses and the children of Israel this song unto the LORD"* — and ⚠ **the reference is off by one as well** (the plate cites xv. 2). **Kept as printed in the transcript**; the English note can now state the reading instead of merely exhibiting the difficulty. **Off the pre-print list.**

## Translator's flags

**Parity 11/11**, line-exact on every page. §2's only hard boundary is the small-caps MISERICORDIA EJUS at the foot of printed 416; the four internal *Peccavi / Peccata* refrains (407, 407 foot, 408) and the numbered pleas 1–9 are movements, not sections.

1. **⚠ SECTION BOUNDARY — a judgment, recorded as such.** The plate gives no all-caps break at 416; what it gives is a **change of kind**. Everything to *Beatus vir qui sperat in Eo* is the sinner speaking — confession, then nine pleas. From the foot of 416 the voice stops and a **schema of God's mercy** begins under small-caps sub-headings (MISERICORDIA EJUS · MISERICORDIA DEI · PATER MISERICORDIARUM) with its own fresh numbered series (*1. Qualis vero in specie* · *2. Lenis in ipsa castigatione* · *3. Placabilitas* · *4. Compassio*, running to printed 419 and beyond) and with **patristic authorities** (August., Chrysos.) among the citations — the tag-and-saying manner of §40's Ἀφορμαί, not of a prayer. I have made that the §2/§3 boundary, as §28's boundary was made and recorded. **It is reversible**: if the numbered mercy-kinds turn out to close by answering the nine pleas, this is one section in two halves, and the files should be merged rather than re-cut.

2. **⚠ PART III IS THE HARLEY RECENSION OF PART II'S PENITENTIAL MATTER — that is now the structural fact of the whole Part.** §1 was a second recension of §23 and §38; §2 is a second recension of **§17** (*Peccatorum Confessio*, printed 323–324) and stands beside **§22/§25/§37**, the three earlier *Confessio Peccati/Peccatorum*. The opening is unmistakable: *conceptus sum in immundo semine · Radix amaritudinis · Surculus oleastri*, all three in §17 too. **Neither recension is conformed to the other.** Where the Latin is identical the English is identical — *Radix amaritudinis* is "A root of bitterness" and *Surculus oleastri* "A shoot of the wild olive" in both, as in §17 — and where it differs, it differs: §17 cites the root of bitterness to `[Heb. xii. 15.]`, Part III to `*Deut.* xxix. 18.` (Hebrews' own source), and both are right. Part III's catalogue is much the longer, running the whole body head to foot.

3. **⚠ THE VOLUME PROVES TWO OF ITS OWN MISPRINTS, and both are kept.**
   - **`Job. xiv. 14.` (406)** should be Job xiv. **4** — and **§17 prints the same sentence with the correct `Job xiv. 4.`**
   - **`Is. xxxiii. 15.` (409)** should be Isaiah xxxviii. **15** — and **§37 prints the same sentence with the correct `[Is. xxxviii. 15.]`** (printed 372).
   This is the strongest evidence in the volume that these are compositor's errors rather than Andrewes' own, and it changes nothing about the rule: **kept as printed, flagged, never mended.** Note too that Part III reads *cum ipse **hæc** fecerim* — once — where §37 has *fecerim, fecerim, fecerim* three times; that is recension, not error, and the English of each follows its own page.

4. **Four more demonstrably wrong references, kept:** `[Marc. iv. 28.]` (410) for Mark iv. **38**, *carest thou not that we perish*; `Jo. ix. 30.` (411) for John ix. **31**; `lxviii. 39.` (411) for Psalm **lxxviii**. 39; `1 Cor. xi. 30.` (413) for 1 Cor. xi. **31**. All four confirmed against the OCR, so none is a misreading of the plate. Six further references are **doubtful and left unadjudicated** because the line is a cento or the miss is a single digit — see transcription note 5.

5. **The Psalter is Coverdale's throughout, per §9, and it pays four times over.** *Out of the deep have I called unto thee, O Lord* and *If thou, Lord, wilt be extreme to mark iniquities* (410, 414) are the BCP's own words for *De profundis* and *Si iniquitates observaveris* — the AV's "If thou, LORD, shouldest mark iniquities" loses *observare* entirely. *What profit is there in my blood, when I go down to the pit* (415) is Coverdale verbatim. **Shall thy wondrous works be known in the dark: and thy righteousness in the land where all things are forgotten** (415) is Coverdale verbatim for a line the plate misnumbers. And at 416 the two lines the Latin splits between *Psal.* xxxiv. 6 and 1 *Pet.* ii. 3 are **one Coverdale verse** — *O taste, and see, how gracious the Lord is: blessed is the man that trusteth in him* — so the English keeps both halves in the Psalter's words and lets the two references stand as printed.
   - One place where Coverdale is deliberately **not** followed: **`Non excuso excusationes in peccato` (412)** is the Vulgate's *ad excusandas excusationes in peccatis*, where the BCP has "let me not be occupied in ungodly works." Andrewes is quoting the Latin idiom and turning it into a denial — *I excuse not excuses in sin* — so the Latin governs and §9 yields, as it yielded at §28 and §40.
   - And one where Coverdale **supplies the argument**: *Quamdiu irasceris populo Tuo? / Supplicanti, preces offerenti?* (412). The BCP's Ps. 80:4 reads "how long wilt thou be angry with thy people **that prayeth**" — the participle the AV drops, and precisely what Andrewes' second line unpacks. Rendered so that the second line reads as the unpacking it is.

6. **`Provoco … a Te ad Te` (410) is a legal appeal, and it is §22's figure.** *Provoco* is the word of Acts xxv. 11, *Cæsarem appello* — to appeal from one court to a higher. §22 (printed 337) sets the same move as a double brace: *{Meritus… / Sed appello…} Domine {a Te justo / ad Te misericordem}*. Here it is spelled out in six lines — from Thee the just to Thee the merciful, from the seat of justice to the throne of grace, from Thee the judge to Thee the Father in Christ — and *placidam provocationem* is **"this peaceable appeal,"** an appeal that does not contest the sentence. The court words are continuous with §7, §32 and §41 and are kept so. **Cross-referenced in both sections; neither imported into the other.**

7. **`Donec ipse non peccator, sed peccatum` (407) — and it is §37's ladder again.** §37's five rungs at printed 377 end *mihi toti peccato → peccato καθ᾽ ὑπερβολήν*: not a sinner but sin itself. Here the same terminus is reached by a different road, through habit (scarlet, sold) and then nature (the leopard, the Ethiopian) — *until I am myself not a sinner, but sin.* The English of the last rung is worded the same in both places; nothing else is carried across.

8. **The dimensions of sin (407 foot–408) are Ephesians iii. 18 again.** *Lata · Dura · Quantitate magna · Longa · Crassa · Profunda · Gravia* — breadth, length, depth — is the measuring figure §14 used of God's mercy (Μῆκος/Πλάτος/Βάθος/Ὕψος, printed 319), turned here upon sin. The English keeps the bare adjectives bare; making them into sentences would lose the survey.

9. **`Quia Christianus.` (408) is the last and worst count, and it is one word.** The series *Quia gratis · cum aviditate · dura fronte · in scandalum · ingratus* ends **Because a Christian** — the aggravation that needs no predicate. Left without one in English, as in the Latin, with Gal. ii. 21 standing beside it.

10. **`calefactus in iniquitate` (406) → "warmed in iniquity."** Not a Vulgate word: the Vulgate of Ps. li. 5 has *in peccatis concepit me mater mea*, but the Hebrew verb (יחמתני) means to be *heated* — the conceiving is a warming. Andrewes has gone behind the Latin to the Hebrew here exactly as at §40's Ps. lxv. 2, and the English keeps the strange word rather than smoothing it back to "conceived." **This is a third quiet override of §9's crib rule**, and for the same reason: where he reaches past the LXX/Vulgate, what he reached for governs.

11. **`Peccatorum primis` (414) is plural, and the plural is the point.** Not "the chief of sinners" as of one man (1 Tim. i. 15) but of two — **Manasseh in the old Testament, Paul in the new**, the pair named two lines above, each the first sinner of his covenant, and the gate of hope opened by their case. English says "the chief of sinners" and lets the flag carry the number, since "the chiefest of sinners" would read as a superlative about degree rather than a pair.

12. **`opus alienum` (416) is Isaiah xxviii. 21, and the whole plea turns on it.** *Misereri opus Tuum · punire et plectere opus alienum* — mercy is God's own work, punishment "his strange work, his strange act." Esaias and Habakkuk are named because they are the two who dare say it. "Strange" is kept for *alienum* against the more obvious "foreign" or "alien," because it is the word the English Bible put into the argument.

13. **`Servus, dissipator, / at servus` (411) and `Etsi nequam, prodigus filius, / at filius tamen`.** Two matched concessions — a servant though a waster, a son though a prodigal — and the English keeps *at … at tamen* as "yet … yet for all that," varying the second only because English cannot say "yet" twice in the same cadence without sounding like an error. *Dissipator* is Luke xvi. 1's *dissipasset bona*, so **"waster,"** not "spendthrift."

14. **`Etiam supplex ultro` (412) → "Yea, and a suppliant unbidden."** *Ultro* = of his own motion, before being asked or compelled — the plea being that he came to beg without waiting to be driven. "Freely" would suggest willingness; "unbidden" keeps the sequence *Thy creature · now wretched · and a suppliant besides*.

15. **The double brace (412).** *{ Dust / Wind / Flesh } { frail / light / dissolved }* — three nouns and three predicates, each brace gathering its own column, so that any of the three answers any of the three. The English keeps the rows and adds no verb; the natural reading runs across (dust is frail, wind is light, flesh dissolves) but the plate deliberately leaves it open, and closing it would be the translator's sentence, not Andrewes'.

16. **The reference column's one-line offset at the foot of 410 is reproduced.** *Psal.* cxix. 94 stands beside *quare serva me* though the sentence begins on the line above, and *Sap.* xi. 25 beside *despexeris* alone. English keeps both references in the same position relative to the text. It looks like a mistake and may be one, but it is the plate's, and re-aligning it would silently make an editorial decision inside the apparatus.

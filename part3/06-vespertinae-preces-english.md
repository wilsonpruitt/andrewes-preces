## Translator's flags

1. **Parity 4/4, line-exact** — 26 / 31 / 30 / 11 against 26 / 31 / 30 / 11. Turned lines reproduced where the English can carry them.

2. **⚠ The chiasm of Psalm xci. 5–6 across the two offices is preserved in the English, and deliberately so** — *Qui liberasti nos a sagitta volante* (433) and *Libera nos a terrore nocturno* (434) are rendered with the same words as their opposite numbers in §5, so that a reader setting the two offices side by side sees the exchange. See the transcript's note 3. **The identical Latin gets identical English; the change of mood (thanksgiving ↔ petition) is the Latin's own and needs no help from the translation.**

3. **⚠ `merebi[tur.]` cannot be shown in English.** Printed 434 has two editorial supplements inside the text: `suum [malum] habuit` and `suum merebi[tur.]`. The first supplies a noun and is carried straight over as **"its own [evil]"**. The second supplies a Latin verb-ending, for which English has no equivalent, and the line is rendered plainly as **"and shall deserve its own"** with no bracket. The transcript carries what the plate carries; this is the loss, and it is recorded rather than disguised.

4. **`Sit somnus meus, ut laboris, sic peccati cessatio` is rendered as a rest from, not a ceasing of.** The Latin is one noun (*cessatio*) governing two genitives; English cannot balance it without an awkward inversion, so "as a rest from labour, so a rest from sin" repeats the noun. The parallel — that sleep stops sinning as it stops working — survives intact, which is the whole point of the line.

5. **`revela mihi aurem` → "uncover mine ear"**, keeping Job xxxiii. 16's figure (*tunc aperit aures virorum*) rather than smoothing it to "open." Andrewes has asked to be visited *visitatione sanctorum* and then for the ear to be uncovered in night-visions; the two clauses are one request and the odd verb is load-bearing.

6. **`et excruciari` → "and to be pierced."** The word is Ps. lxxvii. 6's self-examination (BCP: "I commune with mine own heart, and search out my spirits"), and *excruciari* is far sharper than Coverdale's "commune." §9 yields: the Latin has reached past the Psalter for a harder word and the English follows it.

7. **`increpationem renum` → "the chastening of my reins"** (Ps. xvi. 7, *increpuerunt me renes mei* / BCP "my reins chasten me in the night season"). The BCP's own phrase is used, which keeps the night-setting the Latin assumes.

8. **`In pace in idipsum dormiam` → "In peace, in the selfsame, will I sleep."** The Vulgate's *in idipsum* (Ps. iv. 9) is untranslatable and famous; the Prayer Book drops it altogether ("I will lay me down in peace, and take my rest"). Kept, because the next line's *singulariter* answers it: alone, and yet in the selfsame. **`in spe constituisti me` → "hast set me in hope,"** not the Prayer Book's "makest me dwell in safety" — the Latin says hope and the whole page has been about a sleep that is a rehearsal of death.

9. **`Stramentum vermium` → "The litter of worms."** *Stramentum* is bedding, straw strewn for a beast; against *Lectum sepulchri* two lines above and *In pace … dormiam* two lines below, the word is doing the work of the whole close — the grave is a bed made up, and what it is made up with is worms. "Litter" keeps the strewn bedding; "food of worms" would be a different and lesser sentence.

10. **The last five lines are set to narrow to a point on the page** and the English is fitted to the same funnel — *Into Thy hands, O Lord, / I commend my / spirit, for Thou / hast redeemed me, / O Lord God.* The line-breaks are the plate's, not ours, and they are the last thing the reader of the 1675 book sees. **Any typesetting that reflows them loses the ending.**
